Where did the money go?

Roughly nine of every ten dollars a school district spends is somebody's pay. So “where did spending grow?” is very nearly “whose payroll grew?” This page splits two decades of Iowa City CSD payroll — salaries and benefits — into the jobs that make it up, and asks how much of the growth is simply explained by teaching more children.

The one-line answer. Labor cost rose 163% while enrollment rose 41%. Teachers added the most money ($70M, or 61% of all growth), because it is by far the biggest payroll. But the fastest-growing line was Central administration, which multiplied 4.8× — well over double the 2.6× of the district as a whole. Set against 65% inflation, spending per student rose +13% in real terms.

3. So what actually drove it?

Payroll is the product of three things: how many students there are, how many staff the district employs per student, and what each staff member costs. Splitting the $115M increase across the three:

Read it this way: more students explain a real but minority share of the growth. The district also employs more adults per student than it did in FY2005, and each of those adults costs more — most of which is ordinary wage and benefit inflation rather than a policy choice.

Nominal dollars flatter every twenty-year comparison. In constant FY2025 dollars, spending per student went from $10,970 to $12,391 — +13%. That is the number to quote; the +86% nominal figure mostly measures the dollar, not the district.

Where these numbers come from, and what to distrust

Sources

Two official records, spliced. FY2005–FY2023 is the U.S. Census Bureau's Annual Survey of School System Finances (the F-33), which republishes Iowa's own filing with salaries and benefits broken out by function. FY2024–FY2025 is the Iowa Department of Education's Certified Annual Report workbooks, which the F-33 has not yet caught up to. Staff counts are the federal Common Core of Data. Inflation is BLS CPI-U.

The splice is not a leap of faith: both sources exist for FY2019–FY2023, and on those five years they agree within 0.6% on total labor and essentially to the dollar on every named category. The FY2025 figures were separately reconciled — to the cent — against the district's own certified CAR.

Three things to hold loosely.
1. Teachers vs. paraeducators is modeled, not reported. Neither source separates them — both sit inside “Instruction.” The split here assumes a paraeducator costs 0.40 of a teacher and divides Instruction pay by staff counts. The sensitivity table below shows it barely moves the story, but those two bars are estimates and are tagged as such.
2. Staff headcounts are noisy where the dollars are not. Districts recode roles between years. Two visible cases: reported teacher aides drop about a third at FY2011, and school administrators roughly double at FY2022 — neither with any matching move in spending. The FY2011 one is why the modeled paraeducator line steps down that year; read it as a change in counting, not in staffing. The dollars throughout this page come from the financial filings and are unaffected.
3. Pandemic money distorts the recent years. Federal ESSER funds inflate FY2021–FY2024 payroll and their expiry deflates FY2025. The FY2025 endpoint is a post-ESSER number; treat the last four years as a bump, not a trend.

Further notes on method

Scope. All operating funds, not just the General Fund — including the Management fund, which carries early-retirement and insurance costs that are unmistakably labor and that a General-Fund-only view would miss entirely. Capital funds (SAVE, PPEL, construction) and debt service are excluded; SAVE's construction-management payroll is excluded with them, for consistency with the earlier years.

Unallocated residual. The F-33 does not push every salary dollar into a named function. That remainder is spread pro-rata across the named categories so both halves of the series sit on the same basis. It is under 1% of salaries in every year except FY2005 (2.2% of salaries, 7.7% of benefits) — the base year, so it is worth knowing about.

Transportation looks near-zero because ICCSD contracts the service out: the cost is real but appears as purchased services, not payroll, and so is outside a labor analysis by construction.

Choice of deflator. CPI-U measures what a dollar buys a household, which is the right lens for “did this cost taxpayers more in real terms.” A different question — “did the district pay above the going rate for staff?” — would call for the BLS Employment Cost Index for state and local government compensation, which has run somewhat above CPI over this period. Because the ECI is the larger deflator, an ECI-based series would show less real growth than the +13% shown here. So this page states the higher of the two figures, not the more favorable one.

No grade-level split. A frequent request, deliberately not answered: no public dataset reports district spending by elementary / middle / high school over this period. It could only be modeled from building-level staffing, and would be an allocation, not a measurement.
